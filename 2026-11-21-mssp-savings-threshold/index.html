A high-revenue ACO earns shared savings only past a threshold

How Medicare settles one accountable care organization's year, opened up one step at a time, and how a simulation handles a year that has not happened yet.

Worked example: made-up numbers. The ACO on this page does not exist. I chose its benchmark, size and spending so the arithmetic is easy to follow. I also made it a high-revenue ACO: its providers' own Medicare revenue is high relative to its patients' Medicare spending (42 CFR 425.20). The rules applied to it are the real ones, cited under Sources.

I made this page while building a simulator for the 2027 changes to the Medicare Shared Savings Program. Before settling thousands of simulated years I wanted to be able to settle one by hand. My first draft called 2% the default threshold for a two-sided ACO. The regulation sets no default: each two-sided ACO elects its threshold, and section 3 shows where they ended up.

Two kinds of side notes run through the page. Program notes explain the Medicare side for readers who work in data. Method notes explain the modeling for readers who work in healthcare. Skip whichever you already know.

1. What goes in and what comes out

Once a year Medicare compares what an ACO's patients cost against a target. Two numbers go in: the benchmark, the spending Medicare expected for the ACO's assigned patients, and their actual spending. One number comes out: a shared savings payment to the ACO, or a shared losses bill to it.

Program note · for data readers

An accountable care organization (ACO) is a group of doctors, hospitals and other providers that agrees to be measured on the total Medicare fee-for-service spending of the patients assigned to it. Patients can still see any provider. The ACO's members are paid their normal fees during the year, and at settlement the ACO receives a share of any savings against its benchmark. In two-sided tracks it also repays a share of losses.

2. The same example in numbers

Settlement starts per beneficiary and then scales up by the 20,000 beneficiaries assigned to the ACO. The savings rate divides total savings by the total benchmark, and that rate is what the threshold is checked against.

Written as a rule, with the same colors:

Shared savings=Sharing rate×Savings×(1 − 2% sequestration)ifSavings rate≥MSR, else $0
2026 rules:50% × $7.2M × 98% = $3.53M
2027 rules:60% × $7.2M × 98% = $4.23M
Program note · for data readers

Two limits sit on top of this rule. Payments to BASIC track ACOs are capped at 10% of the benchmark ($24.0M here), and losses are capped as well. Neither cap binds in this example. The full sharing rate also requires the ACO to meet the quality performance standard; this page assumes it does.

3. The threshold

Move the slider to change the ACO's spending. The chart shows the payment at every savings rate. Inside the band around zero, set by the MSR, no money changes hands. Past either edge the payment jumps.

Program note · for data readers

Spending for a fixed group of patients moves from year to year by chance, and the minimum savings rate keeps Medicare from paying for savings that chance alone could produce. One-sided ACOs get a threshold set by their size, from 3.9% at 5,000 assigned beneficiaries down to 2.0% at 60,000 or more, because small populations swing more. Two-sided ACOs like this one elect 0%, 0.5% to 2.0% in half-point steps, or the size-based scale when they start an agreement period or move into a two-sided level during one, and the same rate applies to losses. In the PY2024 results file, 101 of 319 two-sided ACOs have a 0% threshold and 69 have 2.0%. The file reports the rate that applied, so for the largest ACOs it cannot tell a fixed 2% from the size-based scale.

A low-revenue BASIC ACO with at least 5,000 assigned beneficiaries, in an agreement period that began in 2024 or later, can share savings below its threshold at half the rate (42 CFR 425.605(h)). For periods starting in 2027 or later it also needs at least 5,000 in each benchmark year. The ACO on this page is high-revenue, so it is not eligible.

Method note · for healthcare readers

A rule with a jump in it turns a small change in spending into a large change in payment. At 2.0% savings this ACO is paid $2.82M; at 1.92% savings ($4.6M) it is paid $0. So the payment to expect depends on how spread out the possible results are, and section 5 measures that spread by simulation.

4. What the 2027 rule changes for this ACO

For a BASIC Level E ACO that starts a new agreement period in 2027, the final rule raises the sharing rate from 50% to 60%. ACOs in a period that began earlier keep the earlier rules. The MSR and the 30% loss sharing rate do not change. In any year the ACO clears the threshold it is paid more, $0.71M more at 3.0% savings. In any other year the two rule sets give the same result.

The rule also changes how some benchmarks are adjusted, including the prior savings adjustment and the regional adjustment for some ENHANCED ACOs. This page holds the benchmark fixed.

5. Simulating a year that has not happened

Next year's savings rate is unknown. One way to handle that is to treat it as a draw from a range of plausible values and settle the ACO once per draw, under both rule sets. Step through one draw, then five, then 1,000.

Method note · for healthcare readers

A Monte Carlo simulation answers "what could happen" by running the same calculation many times, each time with inputs drawn at random from a stated range. Here only the savings rate is drawn, from a range I chose for the page. A simulation meant to forecast would also draw benchmark trend, spending growth and other inputs, each from a range estimated from data.

Each draw is settled under both rule sets with the same savings rate, a technique called common random numbers. The difference between the two columns then comes from the rule alone. Drawing separately for each rule set would mix the rule's effect with luck in the draws.

Program note · for data readers

The 60.5% describes the made-up range above. A real ACO's odds depend on its own spending history and size.

6. Where the simple version stops

  • One ACO in one track. ENHANCED and the lower BASIC levels have their own sharing rates, loss rates and limits (42 CFR 425.605 and 425.610). The steps are the same and the numbers differ.
  • The benchmark is given. This page takes the benchmark as a number. Medicare arrives at it in several steps of its own, and the 2027 rule changes some of them. That is a separate lesson.
  • Quality and the limits are switched off. The ACO here meets the quality performance standard and stays under the payment limit. An ACO that misses the standard, or saves more than the limit allows, is paid less than this rule gives.
  • The range in section 5 is invented. A forecast needs a range estimated from an ACO's own history. The 60.5% on this page says nothing about any real ACO.
Program note · a common misreading

"An ACO that spends less than its benchmark is paid a share of the difference." For a high-revenue ACO that holds only once the savings rate reaches its minimum savings rate. The ACO on this page can save $4.6M and be paid $0. The band is also a choice for two-sided ACOs: one that elected a 0% threshold has no band, and shares from the first dollar in both directions.

How this was computed

The worked example is made up. The code that settles it is the code I checked against real results: run on the PY2024 Performance Year Financial and Quality Results file with each ACO's reported inputs, it comes within 2% of the reported earned savings or losses for 99.4% of 476 ACOs. The threshold counts in section 3 are from the same file. Every other number on the page is the made-up ACO run through that code.
