My model ranked rural hospital closures well. Why did it expect 49 where 34 happened?

It gives each rural hospital a three-year closure risk, from CMS cost reports (HCRIS) and the Sheps Center's closure list. I tested it on 5,395 hospital-years (one hospital, one cost report year) from 2021 to 2023; 34 preceded a closure, at 17 hospitals. The first check is ranking: in 78% of 182,274 closure and non-closure pairs, the closure had the higher risk. That share is the AUC, 0.780 (0.680 to 0.864 in 90% of resamples of hospitals).

The second check is counting: if the percentages are right, they add up to the closures to expect. The sum was 49.1 against 34 observed, an observed-to-expected ratio of 0.69 (0.41 to 0.99). By report year it expected 14.0, 16.5 and 18.6 and saw 14, 13 and 7; my closure list stops two months before the 2023 window ends. Whether risks match what happens is called calibration; the AUC, using only their order, cannot see it. I rescaled every risk with two numbers fitted on 2019 and 2020. The sum became 32.1, ratio 1.06 (0.63 to 1.52). I had reported that model as meeting my targets.

A review found what I had wrong: 10 of the 20 hospitals closing in the rescaling years' windows count as test-year closures. Refit on 2011 to 2016 and rescaled on 2017 and 2018, which share none, it expects 56.2, ratio 0.61 (0.36 to 0.87). That one over-predicts here and the other had seen part of the answer, so I trust neither count. I would not read a good ranking score as evidence that a 2% risk means 2 in 100.

When a risk model ranks well and its percentages have not held up, what would you still let it decide?
